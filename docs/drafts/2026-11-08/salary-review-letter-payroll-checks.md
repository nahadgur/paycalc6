# Salary Review Letters: What to Check

Before payroll implements a salary review, confirm the new recurring amount, what it includes, the effective date and the first payment date. A letter announcing a percentage increase can still leave payroll uncertain about the figure to enter.

Use the [pay rise calculator](/raise-calculator) after establishing the old and new comparable gross salaries. For the tax arithmetic, read [how much of a pay rise you keep](/blog/how-much-of-a-pay-rise-do-you-keep). This checklist concerns the written instruction behind the change.

## Identify the amount being reviewed

Check whether the letter refers to basic pay, total gross cash pay, net pay or the employer's total package cost. Ask for a breakdown if it does not say. Employer contributions and non-cash benefits should not silently be treated as cash salary.

Write down the old basic salary, each recurring allowance and the corresponding new amounts. Ask which items are unchanged. A higher basic salary accompanied by an absorbed allowance can produce a smaller increase in total cash pay than the headline suggests.

Section 10(5) of the Employment Act requires consultation, revision of the contract to reflect changes to the relevant employment particulars and written notification. Keep the confirmed letter with the employment terms; a payroll entry alone is not a useful record of what was agreed.

## Example: a 10% increase to basic salary

Suppose basic pay is KES 60,000 and a separate recurring allowance is KES 10,000. If the confirmed review increases only basic pay by 10% and leaves the allowance unchanged, the new basic is KES 66,000 and gross cash pay is KES 76,000.

The total increase is KES 6,000 from a previous KES 70,000 gross, or about 8.57%. Entering KES 77,000 would incorrectly apply 10% to the whole former gross amount. These are illustrative contract figures, not a statement that any employee is entitled to an annual 10% rise.

Check the [gross versus net salary explanation](/blog/gross-vs-net-salary-in-kenya) if the employer promises an amount “after tax”. Obtain a clear definition of which deductions are included in that promise before using it in a calculation.

## Separate the effective date from the payment date

A letter may be signed in November but state that the change takes effect in October. Ask whether November's payslip will show both the new recurring salary and a separately identified adjustment for October.

Record the months covered and the old and new rates for each. Follow the [salary arrears checklist](/blog/salary-arrears-and-paye-in-kenya) for a backdated change; do not treat every earlier-period adjustment as an ordinary bonus. If the change starts mid-month, request the agreed proration basis rather than inventing a divisor.

## Check related terms explicitly

Ask whether the change affects pensionable pay, commission targets, overtime calculations or benefits. Do not assume every component automatically tracks basic salary. Where a percentage contribution is involved, identify both the percentage and the pay base.

If a one-off award accompanies the increase, keep it separate from the recurring salary. A large first revised payment can otherwise create an unrealistic expectation of next month's cash. The [salary breakdown guide](/guides/salary-breakdowns) helps interpret those separate lines.

## Reconcile the first revised payslip

Compare the letter with earnings before checking deductions. KRA's PAYE guidance uses progressive income-tax bands; the gross increase is not the same as the extra bank credit. Use the calculator with consistent assumptions and ask payroll about benefit changes or personal deductions that affect the comparison.

Send a focused query if the amount is wrong: identify the confirmed effective date, expected earnings line and actual payslip line. Keep payroll's correction and the revised document. Review the following month as well to ensure a one-off adjustment has not accidentally become recurring salary.
